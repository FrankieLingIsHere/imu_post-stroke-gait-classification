Rojek, I., Mikołajewska, E., Małolepsza, O., Kozielski, M., & Mikołajewski, D. (2025). *Comparative Analysis of Different AI Approaches to Stroke Patients' Gait Analysis*. *Applied Sciences*, 15(20), 10896. https://doi.org/10.3390/app152010896. Verified via a real full-text fetch (both the MDPI page and its PDF, retrieved via a text-extraction proxy after MDPI's own page returned HTTP 403 on every direct WebFetch attempt) and cross-checked against Crossref for the canonical DOI/journal/volume/issue. Found 2026-07-28 via a literature search aimed specifically at [[classification-methods]]'s named classical-vs-deep-learning gap.

## Key findings

- **The closest match found so far to this review's named classical-vs-deep-learning gap**: a single paper trains and evaluates SVM, Random Forest, k-NN, and a four-layer CNN on the identical aggregated spatiotemporal feature set (gait speed, stride length, step length, cadence, and their leg-length-normalized versions), the identical 120-patient stroke population, and the identical binary task, normal-versus-hemiplegic gait pattern.
- Accuracy: CNN 91.88% (RMSE 0.001), SVM 89.91% (RMSE 0.01), k-NN 87.52% (RMSE 0.02), Random Forest 85.41% (RMSE 0.02). Only the CNN gets a full per-class breakdown reported (precision 91.12, recall 91.12, F1 91.88) — the other three models' precision/recall/F1 are not individually broken out in the available text, only their overall accuracy and RMSE.
- The authors' own conclusion: CNNs "outperform traditional ML methods in capturing complex gait patterns," while classical methods retain an interpretability and computational-efficiency advantage — directly consistent with [[classification-methods]]'s existing verdict that classical methods are the more plausible near-term choice while deep learning remains promising-but-less-tested, rather than contradicting it.
- **One real, unresolved caveat keeps this from being a fully clean IC1-style stroke-vs-healthy comparison or an unambiguous placement report**: data collection combined wearable IMUs, pressure sensors, and video recording in parallel, with a gold-standard motion-capture system used as a reference for only a subset of the data. The available text never states explicitly which of these streams produced the six spatiotemporal parameters actually fed into the four classifiers, and no IMU count or anatomical placement is given anywhere. A wearable IMU component is clearly present and part of primary data collection, which is why this is filed as IC4 rather than excluded, but the exact provenance of the features themselves is a real reporting gap, not an assumption resolved in this study's favor.
- No separate healthy control cohort was recruited — "normal" gait is defined by comparison against literature reference ranges, which is why this sits on the IC4 pathway (stroke participants only, graded/binary outcome) rather than IC1.
- [[quality-assessment]]: held-out evaluation confirmed (70/20/10 train/test/validation split plus k-fold cross-validation, explicitly described as being "to prevent overfitting"). No code availability. Data "available from the authors upon request" only. Neither chronicity nor a standardized severity score is reported beyond the coarse 3-month-to-3-year post-stroke range for the whole convenience sample. No control-group matching statement, since no control group was recruited. The authors themselves self-disclose the small-sample-size limitation and note that "models were trained on aggregated gait parameters rather than raw sensor or video data, which reduces the richness of the analysis" — a direct author-stated caveat, not one uncovered by this review.
