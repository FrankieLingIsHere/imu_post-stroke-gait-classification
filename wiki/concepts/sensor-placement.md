Where on the body a sensor should sit — [[research-questions|RQ2]]. This review's own re-mining of both real paired stroke datasets is its strongest placement evidence, and the supporting literature is genuinely mixed rather than a clean five-study convergence, **corrected 2026-07-29 (twice)** after two successive `journal-critic` adversarial reviews flagged first the overclaiming framing and then a specific miscited study (see below). The manuscript's Section 5.3 now opens with **Figure 4** (renumbered 2026-07-21, see [[quality-assessment]] for why), a qualitative synthesis map covering every study and dataset with a placement finding — deliberately not an accuracy comparison, since placements are heterogeneous in task, population, and validation design (Section 3.7).

## The trunk-placement evidence, corrected 2026-07-29 (twice)

**Extended 2026-08-05 (wiki-ingest pass)**: three further studies test a trunk-region sensor alone, found via the same IEEE Xplore/Scopus supplementary search that added six studies to the manuscript — [[iosa-2021]] (single sacral S1-S2 IMU, 90.9% stroke-vs-healthy accuracy), [[tas-2024]] (single waist/lumbosacral accelerometer, 91% Brunnstrom-stage accuracy), and [[igarashi-2024]] (single L3 trunk accelerometer, IC5-exempt threshold analysis, AUC 0.833 vertical/0.819 mediolateral distinguishing ambulation-dependent from -independent stroke inpatients — this review's most direct external corroboration of the trunk-RMS finding, per Section 5.1). Like the three studies below, none of these three runs a placement comparison within its own study — the same "independent success at that placement, not comparative evidence" caveat applies to all six now, not three.

Three included studies report strong accuracy from a lower-back or lumbar sensor used alone — [[hsu-2021]], [[lee-2018]], and [[inui-2026]] — but **none of the three compared that placement against an alternative within its own study**. Their contribution is independent success at that placement, not comparative evidence that it beats another site. **[[inui-2026]]'s result specifically comes from an uneven-surface walking protocol** (added 2026-07-30), not standard overground gait, a task difference worth flagging separately from the placement question. [[mannini-2016]] previously appeared on this list as a fourth study; a second `journal-critic` review caught that this was wrong, verified directly against the full text: Mannini's seven-variable HMM feature vector draws six variables from a single shank sensor and only one from the waist, with no lumbar-only result reported anywhere. It is shank-dominant, not lumbar-placement evidence, and was removed from this list. **Corrected again 2026-07-30, fourth `journal-critic` pass**: that removal over-corrected. Mannini *does* use a genuine lumbar sensor (L4–S2) — the accurate statement is that it doesn't produce lumbar-*dominant* evidence, not that the sensor itself isn't real. [[abdollahi-2024]] is the one literature study that does run a genuine placement comparison in this direction, finding a single thorax sensor performing comparably to its own 8-sensor array — a nearby but distinct site from lumbar or lower back, not the same one.

Two other included studies ran a genuine multi-placement stroke comparison and did **not** favor the trunk: [[hsu-2018]] found the shank outperforming lower back (for a different classification target, see below), and [[pohl-2022]] found ankle-inclusive configurations outperforming wrist-only input, neither result centered on trunk placement. [[brasiliano-2026]], the largest multi-placement comparison in the review (five simultaneous placements, 85 vs 97 participants), does not report which placement(s) produced the nine features its own sequential backward selection retained — a genuine, unresolved reporting gap, not a finding either for or against trunk placement.

This review's own re-mining of [[voisard-2025]] and [[felius-dataset]] runs three matched RMS comparisons rather than crowning a single winning placement, **rewritten 2026-08-01 to report findings side by side instead of ranking them**: lower back RMS shows an age-adjusted effect of r = 0.57 in Voisard, head RMS a smaller r = 0.31, and foot RMS a larger r = 0.72 — Felius's own independent raw comparison (different hardware, different protocol) shows the same foot-larger-than-lower-back ordering, r = 0.79 vs. r = 0.61. Voisard and Felius are not independent of each other in one sense, since both are processed by this review's own single analysis pipeline, but they are independently collected data on independent populations with different hardware. "Age-independent" would overstate the lower-back finding on its own: only Voisard was actually age-adjusted (the one dataset with participant-level demographics); Felius corroborates the same direction only in its own raw, unadjusted comparison, since its public release carries no demographic data to adjust for age at all. **In short**: no genuinely independent third-party literature result currently corroborates the lower-back-specific finding on its own terms — lower back's advantage over *head* placement is well supported by this review's own re-mining and by one literature comparison in a nearby site (thorax), but lower back is not established as beating foot, and is not the uniform verdict of the multi-placement literature as a whole either.

**Foot-vs-lower-back matched comparison, findings listed not ranked (updated 2026-08-01)**: this page and the manuscript previously framed lower back placement as "this review's own strongest placement evidence" before conceding foot's larger effect size in a subordinate clause — a real asymmetry a later check found, not just a word-choice nicety, since the two findings differ in *scope* as much as in raw effect size and neither should be read as simply outranking the other. Foot RMS was tested only within the two real stroke datasets (not against Section 4.2.6's broader five-dataset healthy-reference generalization check, since this review's own extraction code does not currently compute foot RMS for those five datasets). Lower-back RMS was tested at that broader scope and generalized there. The current, correct participant-level figures (refreshed 2026-08-01 after excluding a duplicated Felius participant, see [[felius-dataset]]) are: Voisard foot r = 0.72 vs. lower back r = 0.57; Felius foot r = 0.79 vs. lower back r = 0.61. Lower back's advantage over *head* placement in Voisard still holds on its own matched RMS-vs-RMS comparison. The manuscript, this page, and the Abstract/Conclusion all now report the foot and lower-back findings as two separate, differently-scoped results rather than calling either one "this review's own strongest placement evidence" outright.

**Mechanism, corrected 2026-07-30 (fourth `journal-critic` pass)**: the original framing here asserted, as settled fact, that a lower back or thorax sensor captures whole-body postural and balance-related compensation directly (trunk lean, pelvic drop) rather than only the mechanics of the affected limb. That mechanism predicts *higher* trunk RMS in stroke participants. This review's own re-mining finds the opposite in both Voisard and Felius: stroke participants show *lower* trunk RMS than healthy participants (positive rank-biserial r under this review's sign convention — see [[discriminative-features]]). A markedly slower walking speed in the stroke cohorts is a more parsimonious alternative explanation, less forceful steps producing smaller trunk acceleration regardless of any compensatory movement pattern, but gait speed itself was never measured directly or tested as a mediator. Neither explanation is confirmed over the other. The mechanism is genuinely open, not settled, and should be read that way in any deployment discussion drawing on it.

## Raw magnitude and discriminative value track together here

[[duo-gait]]'s 9-sensor, 6-site leaderboard (corrected 2026-08-01: feet ~1.97g, not the stale ~1.40g this page previously carried, which never matched a fresh recomputation and was inconsistent with Camargo's own independent foot figure below) finds feet highest for raw movement magnitude, wrists intermediate (~1.17g), trunk and head lowest (~1.02-1.06g). This now points the *same* direction as the discriminative-value finding above, not the opposite one: feet carry both the richest raw dynamic range and, on this review's own re-mining, the larger RMS-based effect size. [[camargo-2021]], unblocked 2026-07-22, corroborates the same raw-magnitude ordering independently: foot 2.10g, shank 1.59g, thigh 1.36g, trunk 1.33g.

## Convergent literature, not a disagreement

[[hsu-2018]] finds **shank** placement reaching the highest accuracy, ahead of foot, ahead of thigh/lower back — for classifying stroke against *other neurological disorders*, not against healthy controls. **Updated 2026-08-01**: earlier versions of this page framed this as "the one open disagreement" with this review's own re-mining, on the assumption that Voisard/Felius showed lower back leading. Now that the correct, participant-level Voisard/Felius numbers show foot beating lower back too, Hsu's own ordering (shank > foot > lower back) points the *same* direction as this review's own finding, not a conflicting one. Both point toward a distal placement matching or exceeding lower back rather than lower back being uniquely superior. Still read as convergent rather than confirmed as the identical underlying effect, since the two studies use different classification targets and no dataset audited here tests both targets with the same placements to settle that directly. See [[future-directions]].

## Shank-placement literature, added 2026-08-05 (wiki-ingest pass)

Two further studies from the same IEEE Xplore/Scopus supplementary search test a shank placement alone, corroborating [[hsu-2018]]'s own shank-leading finding rather than adding a fourth independent site: [[shih-2021]] (bilateral shank IMU, 99.04% stroke-vs-healthy accuracy via k-fold CV, and this review's only cross-system/cross-dataset external-generalization evidence — the same trained model held above 98% against an optical motion-capture cohort and the independent PAMAP2 public dataset) and [[orfanos-2026]] (bilateral shank gyroscope-only features, best Random Forest F1=0.94, AUC=0.98). Neither runs a multi-placement comparison within its own study, so both are read the same way as the trunk-alone studies above — independent success at the shank specifically, not comparative placement evidence — but their convergence with [[hsu-2018]]'s comparative result strengthens the distal-placement pattern this page's "Convergent literature" section below already describes.

## Wrist and pocket findings

[[marea]] finds wrist noisier and higher-variance than waist/ankle (arm-swing variability). [[pohl-2022]] finds wrist-only input underperforming ankle-inclusive configurations in a real stroke cohort — converging with the MAREA finding despite different populations. [[oxwalk]] finds hip and wrist agree closely (same underlying annotated steps, a pipeline sanity check more than a placement finding). [[shin-2022]] and [[lee-2025]] are the two included studies testing pocket placement directly in stroke participants. **Flagged 2026-07-30**: Lee et al.'s (2025) own stroke and healthy cohorts differ in age by roughly 37 years, unaddressed in the study's own analysis, the same class of confound this review's own age-adjustment work above was built to catch, so its pocket-placement result should be read with that caveat attached.

[[camargo-2021]] documents the widest simultaneous-placement range of any audited dataset. **Unblocked 2026-07-22** (see that page for the full access-history correction) — no longer excluded from signal-derived comparison. Across 880 level-ground trials from all 22 subjects, it corroborates [[duo-gait]]'s foot-highest, trunk-lowest raw-magnitude ordering with a second, entirely different dataset and protocol: foot 2.10g, shank 1.59g, thigh 1.36g, trunk 1.33g.
